## M1-S2-02 — HRBA in Plain Language

A Human Rights-Based Approach is a practical way of designing and implementing CSO work so that people are treated as rights-holders and responsible actors are recognized as duty-bearers.

In simple terms, HRBA helps CSOs:

- place people’s dignity at the center;
- include people who are often left out;
- support meaningful participation;
- strengthen accountability;
- connect needs to rights and responsibilities;
- improve how projects are designed, implemented, monitored, and learned from.

HRBA does not mean every CSO must become a legal organization. It means CSOs use human rights principles to improve everyday practice.

### Statement

HRBA helps CSOs move from “we are helping people” to “we are working with rights-holders to strengthen dignity, participation, and accountability.”

## M1-S2-03 — Why HRBA Matters in Everyday CSO Work

HRBA matters because it can improve how CSOs plan, decide, listen, act, monitor, and learn.

### Card 2 — Participation

## M1-S2-04 — Activity-Focused vs Rights-Aware Practice

Two CSOs may run similar activities, but the approach can be very different.

### Activity-focused practice asks:

What activity will we deliver?  
How many people will attend?  
What outputs will we report?

### Rights-aware practice also asks:

Who is affected and who is excluded?  
What rights are connected to this issue?  
Who has responsibility?  
How did people participate in the decision?  
How will feedback be handled safely?  
What changes in dignity, access, inclusion, or accountability?

### Key takeaway

HRBA does not remove activities. It makes activities more meaningful, inclusive, and accountable.

# Section 3 — The First HRBA Shift

## M1-S3-01 — From Beneficiaries to Rights-Holders

One of the first HRBA shifts is the move from seeing people only as beneficiaries to recognizing them as rights-holders.

### Beneficiary framing

People are mainly seen as recipients of support.

Common question:  
“What help do they need?”

### Rights-holder framing

People are recognized as persons and groups with rights, voice, agency, and legitimate claims.

Common questions:  
“What rights are connected to this issue?”  
“What barriers prevent people from enjoying these rights?”  
“Who has responsibility to respond?”  
“How can people participate meaningfully?”

### Key message

The word “beneficiary” may still appear in donor or project language. But HRBA invites us to think more deeply: people are not only recipients of services; they are rights-holders.

## M1-S3-02 — From Service Delivery Alone to Empowerment and Accountability

Service delivery can be important. CSOs often provide valuable support, information, resources, training, protection, referrals, or community services.

HRBA does not reject service delivery.

Instead, HRBA asks whether service delivery also strengthens:

- participation;
- inclusion;
- empowerment;
- accountability;
- access to information;
- dignity;
- the capacity of people to claim rights;
- the capacity of responsible actors to fulfill obligations.

### Statement

A rights-based CSO does not only ask, “Did we deliver the activity?” It also asks, “Did this activity strengthen people’s dignity, participation, and ability to claim their rights safely?”

## M1-S3-04 — What Changes When We Ask Rights-Based Questions?

When a CSO asks rights-based questions early, several things can change.

### Planning changes

The team may identify groups that were missing from the first plan.

### Participation changes

The team may adjust the time, language, venue, or facilitation method so more people can participate safely.

### Accountability changes

The team may invite relevant responsible actors or clarify how community feedback will be shared and followed up.

### Evidence changes

The team may collect safer, more useful information that shows barriers, not only activity counts.

### Power changes

The team may shift from speaking for people toward helping people speak, organize, and influence decisions.

# Section 4 — Charity, Needs, Service Delivery, and Rights-Based Practice

## M1-S4-01 — Four Ways CSOs May Frame Their Work

CSO work can be framed in different ways. Each framing may contain something useful, but HRBA goes further.

### Charity-based framing

Focus: giving help to people in need.  
Typical question: What can we give?  
Limit: people may be treated as passive recipients.

### Needs-based framing

Focus: identifying needs and gaps.  
Typical question: What do people lack?  
Limit: needs may be addressed without asking about rights, responsibility, or accountability.

### Service-delivery framing

Focus: delivering activities and services.  
Typical question: What service will we provide?  
Limit: delivery may happen without meaningful participation or feedback.

### Rights-based framing

Focus: dignity, rights, participation, inclusion, and accountability.  
Typical question: What rights are affected, who is excluded, and who has responsibility?  
Strength: people are recognized as rights-holders with voice and agency.

## M1-S4-03 — Why the Rights-Based Option Goes Further

Charity, needs assessment, and service delivery can all be valuable. But by themselves, they may not change the deeper conditions that keep people excluded or unheard.

Rights-based practice goes further because it asks:

- Are people involved in decisions that affect them?
- Are excluded groups being deliberately included?
- Are rights and responsibilities clear?
- Can people raise concerns safely?
- Are duty-bearers engaged where appropriate?
- Is the CSO strengthening dignity and accountability, not only delivering activities?

# Section 5 — Rights-Holders, Duty-Bearers, and the Role of CSOs

## M1-S5-01 — Rights-Holders and Duty-Bearers

HRBA uses two important terms: rights-holders and duty-bearers.

### Rights-holders

Rights-holders are people or groups who have rights. All people are rights-holders.

In CSO work, rights-holders may include community members, women, young people, persons with disabilities, children, older persons, workers, displaced people, minority groups, service users, or any group affected by an issue.

### Duty-bearers

Duty-bearers are actors with obligations or responsibilities to respect, protect, and fulfill rights.

The state is usually the main duty-bearer. Depending on the issue, duty-bearers may include public offices, service providers, local authorities, schools, health facilities, justice actors, or other responsible institutions.

### CSO role

CSOs are usually not the primary duty-bearer. But they can play an important role by supporting rights-holders, strengthening participation, generating evidence, facilitating dialogue, monitoring commitments, and promoting accountability safely.

## M1-S5-04 — Where Do CSOs Fit?

CSOs can help connect rights-holders and duty-bearers in constructive, safe, and accountable ways.

### CSOs may support rights-holders by:

- sharing accessible information;
- creating safe participation spaces;
- strengthening community voice;
- helping people understand rights and responsibilities;
- documenting barriers safely;
- supporting collective action where appropriate.

### CSOs may engage duty-bearers by:

- sharing evidence;
- facilitating dialogue;
- asking for responses;
- tracking commitments;
- advocating for inclusive services;
- promoting transparency and accountability.

## M1-S5-05 — Common Misunderstanding

### Myth

“If we are a CSO, then we are always the duty-bearer.”

### Reality

Not quite. CSOs have responsibilities to work ethically, safely, and accountably. But in HRBA, the state is usually the main duty-bearer for human rights obligations.

### Myth

“HRBA means only advocacy CSOs need to care about rights.”

### Reality

Not quite. HRBA can strengthen many types of CSO work, including service delivery, community development, protection, social accountability, gender equality, youth work, disability inclusion, MEAL, and peacebuilding.

### Myth

“Participation means inviting people to attend an event.”

### Reality

Not quite. Attendance is not enough. Meaningful participation means people can influence decisions that affect them, especially groups often excluded.

## M1-S6-07 — Domain Feedback

Review your domain scores. Each domain is scored out of 6.

### Participation

This domain looks at whether people affected by the work can influence plans and whether barriers to participation are reduced.

If low: Start involving affected groups before major decisions are made. Add a simple participation checklist to planning.

### Non-discrimination and equality

This domain looks at whether your CSO identifies exclusion and adjusts activities when some groups benefit less.

If low: Identify who may be excluded, facing barriers, or benefiting less. Adjust outreach and support accordingly.

### Accountability

This domain looks at whether people can raise questions, concerns, or complaints safely and whether responses are tracked.

If low: Make feedback and complaints channels clearer, safer, and more trusted. Track whether responses are completed.

### Transparency

This domain looks at whether your CSO shares objectives, criteria, decisions, progress, and changes in plain language.

If low: Share objectives, criteria, decisions, and progress in plain language. Close the feedback loop with communities.

### Empowerment

This domain looks at whether people understand rights, services, and decision-making spaces and can speak for themselves.

If low: Link activities to relevant rights or standards without unnecessary jargon. Check access, acceptability, and quality.
