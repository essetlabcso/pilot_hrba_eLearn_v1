Welcome. In this lesson, we will explore how context and inequality analysis helps CSOs design stronger, more rights-based projects
In Module 2, you practiced the Everyday Rights Lens. You looked at questions of information, access, voice, inclusion, responsibility, response, participation, accountability, and power.
Now, in Module 3, you are moving into project design.
And this is where context and inequality analysis becomes essential.
Before a CSO chooses activities, writes objectives, builds a logframe, or proposes indicators, it needs to ask a deeper question:
What is really happening, who is affected differently, and why?
A needs-based project may begin with what is visible. For example: the market needs improvement, the health post needs renovation, the water point needs repair, or young people need training.
Those needs may be real. But HRBA asks us to look further.
It asks:
 Who uses the market, and who may be pushed out if fees increase?
 Who can reach the health post, and who cannot?
 Who waits longer for water, who pays more, and who has less voice in decisions?
 Who attended the consultation, and who actually influenced the final priorities?
Context analysis is not just background information. It is the foundation for better project design.
It helps a CSO understand the political, social, cultural, economic, environmental, and institutional factors that shape the problem. It also helps identify the rights at stake, the responsibilities involved, and the barriers that make some groups more excluded than others.
Inequality analysis goes one step deeper.
It asks whether people are affected in the same way or differently. It looks at gender, disability, age, income, location, language, displacement, social status, livelihood, care responsibilities, and other factors that may change people’s experience of the same problem.
For example, a plan may say: “The community needs a modern market.”
But for women informal vendors, market modernization may mean higher fees, stricter rules, loss of selling space, or less control over income.
For persons with disabilities, a renovated public building may still remain inaccessible if ramps, information formats, seating arrangements, or transport barriers are not considered.
For young people, a training activity may look useful, but it may not respond to the real issue if they have no voice in local decisions, no safe way to raise concerns, or no link to livelihoods.
For remote kebeles, the problem may not only be service quality. It may be distance, transport cost, language, information gaps, or weak follow-up from duty-bearers.
This is why context and inequality analysis matters for HRBA-aligned project design.
It helps the CSO avoid designing for “the community” as if everyone has the same situation, the same voice, and the same risk.
It helps the project move from a general activity idea to a more precise rights-based response.
A strong context and inequality scan asks four practical questions.
First: What is happening?
 This means describing the visible problem clearly, without jumping too quickly to a solution. What is the situation? What evidence exists? What do people say? What is already known?
Second: Who is affected differently?
 This means looking beyond average experience. Which groups are most affected? Which groups are present but unheard? Which groups may be invisible in official records or meeting attendance lists?
Third: What barriers may exist?
 Barriers can be physical, financial, social, cultural, institutional, informational, or related to power and safety. For example, a meeting may be open to everyone, but women vendors may not speak if the agenda is already fixed. Persons with disabilities may be invited but unable to participate if the venue, information, or timing is inaccessible.
Fourth: What evidence should be verified safely?
 HRBA does not mean making strong claims without evidence. It means asking better questions and checking information carefully. Safe evidence may include disaggregated service data, observation, community feedback, policy commitments, accessibility checks, budget information, or separate discussions with groups who may not speak freely in public meetings.
But evidence must be handled carefully.
Do not collect real names, sensitive complaints, exact locations, survivor stories, or identifiable details unless there is a clear and safe reason, consent, and protection system. For this course, use fictional, generalized, or non-sensitive examples.
For local CSOs, context and inequality analysis is also a strength. Local organizations often understand informal systems, local power relations, social norms, service barriers, and community trust better than external actors. But this knowledge needs to be organized carefully so that it can inform project design.
A good scan does not only say, “people need services.”
It says:
 which rights are affected,
 which groups are affected differently,
 which duty-bearers or service actors have responsibilities,
 which barriers explain the problem,
 which evidence is missing,
 and what design choices should change.
In the Jiru Amba case, the plan looks active. There are consultations, activities, budgets, and indicators. But the question is not only whether the plan is busy. The question is whether it understands exclusion.
Did the women vendors influence the market priorities?
 Were persons with disabilities able to shape the design of public services?
 Were youth consulted in a way that gave them real influence?
 Were remote kebeles reached?
 Were duty-bearer responsibilities clear?
 Were risks and accountability gaps considered before activities were selected?
This is the purpose of the Context and Inequality Scan.
It slows the design process down at the right moment.
It helps you avoid choosing activities too early.
It helps you see what is missing before the project becomes fixed.
And it helps your CSO design responses that are more inclusive, more accountable, more realistic, and more likely to produce meaningful change.
As you complete the scan, do not try to write a perfect analysis. Start with practical observations.
Ask:
 What do we know?
 Who may be affected differently?
 What barriers may be hidden?
 What do we need to verify safely?
 And what should this change in the project design?
That is the heart of HRBA-aligned context and inequality analysis.
It is not a separate academic exercise.
It is the first step toward a better project.
