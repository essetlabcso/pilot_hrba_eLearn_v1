## M2-S2-02 — Human Rights in Plain Language

Human rights are basic rights and freedoms that belong to every person because they are human.

They are connected to:

- dignity;
- equality;
- freedom;
- safety;
- participation;
- access to services and opportunities;
- the possibility to live a decent life.

Human rights help CSOs ask practical questions:

- Are people being treated with dignity?
- Are some groups excluded?
- Can people participate in decisions that affect them?
- Do people have information they can understand?
- Are services accessible and acceptable for different groups?
- Are responsible actors meeting their responsibilities?
- What needs to change so people can enjoy their rights in practice?

### Key message

Human rights are not benefits that CSOs give. People already have rights. CSOs can help make those rights more visible, practical, and respected.

# Section 3 — Human Rights in Everyday Life

## M2-S3-01 — Everyday Issues Can Have Rights Dimensions

A CSO may start with a simple issue:

“People are not attending community meetings.”

At first, this may look like a participation problem only. But a rights lens helps the CSO ask deeper questions.

Maybe people did not receive information in time.

Maybe the meeting place is not accessible.

Maybe some groups do not feel safe speaking.

Maybe women with care responsibilities cannot attend at that time.

Maybe young people are not invited.

Maybe people gave feedback before and never received a response.

An everyday issue can reveal several rights dimensions.

### Rights dimensions to notice

- participation;
- equality and non-discrimination;
- access to information;
- safety and dignity;
- accessibility of services or spaces;
- accountability;
- voice in decisions;
- quality and acceptability of support.

# Section 4 — Characteristics of Human Rights

## M2-S4-01 — Why Rights Characteristics Matter

Human rights have important characteristics.

These characteristics help CSOs avoid common mistakes.

For example, a CSO should not assume that only some groups deserve dignity, that participation can be ignored because time is short, or that service delivery is enough if people cannot access information or raise concerns safely.

Human rights characteristics help us remember that rights belong to people, are connected, and should be applied in practical and inclusive ways.

### Button

Continue

## M2-S4-02 — Four Characteristics of Human Rights

Open each card to review a key characteristic.

### Card 1 — Universal

Human rights belong to all people.

A CSO should not design support as if only some groups deserve dignity, voice, information, safety, or fair treatment.

### Card 2 — Inalienable

People do not lose their rights because they are poor, displaced, young, old, unpopular, undocumented, difficult to reach, or excluded.

Rights are not gifts given only to people who are easy to serve.

### Card 3 — Indivisible

Different rights matter together.

Civil, political, economic, social, and cultural rights should not be treated as if one group of rights is always important and another group can be ignored.

### Card 4 — Interdependent

The enjoyment of one right often depends on other rights.

For example, participation may depend on information, safety, mobility, language, accessibility, and confidence.

### Completion message

These characteristics help CSOs think beyond single activities and notice connected barriers.

## M2-S4-03 — One Right Affects Another

A CSO is supporting children who are out of school.

At first, the team treats the issue only as a lack of school materials. They plan to provide notebooks and pens.

Then the team applies a rights lens and asks deeper questions:

- Are children able to access school safely?
- Are girls and boys affected differently?
- Are children with disabilities able to participate?
- Do families have information about available support?
- Are there language, cost, distance, or safety barriers?
- Who has responsibility to respond?
- How can children and families participate safely in understanding the issue?

The team realizes that education may be connected to information, equality, safety, accessibility, participation, and accountability.

### Key message

Rights are connected. A practical CSO response should look beyond the first visible need and ask what barriers affect people’s dignity and access in real life.

Human rights are often grouped into broad types.

These categories help us organize our thinking. But real life is not always separated into neat categories.

For example, a community meeting may involve:

- participation;
- access to information;
- freedom of expression;
- non-discrimination;
- accessibility;
- safety;
- accountability.

## M2-S5-02 — Types of Rights in CSO Practice

Open each tab to explore broad types of rights and how they may appear in CSO work.

### Tab 1 — Civil rights

Civil rights often relate to safety, privacy, fair treatment, and protection from arbitrary harm.

In CSO work, this may appear when people need to participate without fear, receive respectful treatment, or avoid unsafe exposure of personal information.

### Tab 2 — Political rights

Political rights often relate to participation, association, expression, and public decision-making.

In CSO work, this may appear when communities participate in planning, speak about issues affecting them, join associations, or engage local decision-makers.

### Tab 3 — Economic rights

Economic rights often relate to work, livelihood, fair conditions, and social protection.

In CSO work, this may appear in livelihood projects, economic inclusion, skills development, decent work, or social support.

### Tab 4 — Social rights

Social rights often relate to education, health, housing, water, food, and basic services.

In CSO work, this may appear in service access, referral systems, school participation, health information, or support for people facing barriers.

### Tab 5 — Cultural rights

Cultural rights often relate to language, identity, cultural life, and participation in community life.

In CSO work, this may appear when information must be accessible in local languages, when community norms affect participation, or when cultural identity shapes access and dignity.

### Tab 6 — Collective rights

Collective rights relate to groups and communities, including shared interests, resources, and collective participation.

In CSO work, this may appear in community dialogue, local resource decisions, environmental concerns, displacement, or collective advocacy.

## M2-S5-04 — Why the Type Matters, But Does Not Limit the Issue

Knowing the type of right can help a CSO choose better questions.

For example:

- if participation is involved, ask who is included in decisions;
- if access to services is involved, ask who can and cannot access support;
- if information is involved, ask whether people understand the message;
- if equality is involved, ask who benefits less or faces barriers;
- if accountability is involved, ask how feedback is received and acted on.

But do not stop with one label.

A rights-based approach asks how different rights connect in real life.

### Key message

Rights types help you organize thinking. They should not stop you from seeing the whole picture.

# Section 6 — Human Rights Systems and Standards

## M2-S6-01 — What Are Human Rights Systems and Standards?

Human rights are supported by systems and standards.

These may include:

- international human rights standards;
- African human rights standards;
- national laws and policies;
- recommendations from human rights institutions or review processes;
- organizational policies and commitments;
- community accountability standards.

For CSO practice, standards can help us ask better questions.

They can help us understand:

- what rights may be involved;
- who may be excluded;
- what responsible actors should consider;
- what quality, access, participation, and accountability should look like;
- how CSO work can avoid harm and strengthen dignity.

### Important note

This course supports learning and practical reflection. It does not provide legal advice.

## M2-S6-03 — Standards Are Tools, Not Shortcuts

Human rights standards can guide CSO work, but they should be used carefully.

In this course, use standards to:

- ask better questions;
- identify barriers;
- notice exclusion;
- strengthen participation;
- improve accountability;
- connect services to dignity and access;
- support safer analysis and referral where needed.

Do not use standards in this course to:

- make unsupported legal claims about real cases;
- publicly accuse people or institutions;
- expose confidential information;
- replace legal advice;
- replace community participation;
- rank CSOs publicly;
- create risk for rights-holders, communities, staff, or organizations.

### Statement

Standards are not shortcuts. They are guides for safer, clearer, and more accountable practice.

# Section 7 — Connecting Human Rights to CSO Practice

## M2-S7-01 — From Rights Idea to CSO Action

A rights lens becomes useful when it improves CSO decisions.

Use this simple process to connect human rights to everyday work.

What is happening? What problem, barrier, or concern is visible?

### Step 2 — Identify rights dimensions

Which rights dimensions may be involved: dignity, equality, participation, information, safety, access, quality, or accountability?

### Step 3 — Identify affected groups

Who is affected? Who may be less visible? Who may face barriers?

### Step 4 — Identify responsible actors

Who has responsibility, obligation, influence, or capacity to respond?

### Step 5 — Identify CSO contribution

What can your CSO do safely and constructively?

### Step 6 — Identify learning evidence

How will you know whether practice is becoming more rights-based?

### Key message

A rights lens does not replace CSO project planning. It improves planning by making dignity, exclusion, participation, responsibility, and accountability more visible.

## M2-S7-02 — A Feedback Box Is Not Enough

A CSO places a feedback box at a project site.

At first, the team believes this is enough for accountability.

Later, they realize:

- many community members cannot read the form;
- some people fear retaliation;
- some people do not know what happens after submitting feedback;
- some groups rarely visit the project site;
- staff are not sure who reviews the feedback;
- people rarely hear what changed because of feedback.

A rights-based approach asks:

- Is feedback accessible?
- Is it safe?
- Are people informed?
- Does the CSO respond?
- Are excluded groups heard?
- Is feedback changing practice?

### Key message

Accountability is not only collecting feedback. It is listening, responding, explaining, and changing practice where needed.

## M2-S7-05 — Scenario Decision: The Most Rights-Based Next Step

### Scenario

Lomi Community Development Association is planning a consultation on improving access to local services.

The team has prepared a meeting agenda and wants to invite community representatives. Ayele notices that some groups are often missing from similar meetings, including people with disabilities, women with care responsibilities, young people, and people from remote areas.

The team wants to take a more rights-aware next step.

### Question

What is the most rights-based next step?

A. Continue with the existing meeting plan because inviting some representatives is enough.  
B. Pause and ask who may be excluded, what barriers exist, what information people need, and how the meeting can be made more accessible and safe.  
C. Cancel the consultation because inclusion is too difficult.  
D. Collect personal details from every person in the community before making any decision.

Best response: B

Feedback for A: Not quite. Inviting some representatives may be useful, but HRBA asks whether participation is meaningful and who may still be excluded.  
Feedback for B: That’s right. This response applies a rights lens by asking about exclusion, barriers, information, accessibility, safety, and participation before moving ahead.  
Feedback for C: Not quite. Inclusion can be challenging, but HRBA encourages practical adjustments rather than abandoning participation.  
Feedback for D: Not quite. HRBA does not mean collecting as much personal data as possible. Data should be safe, necessary, and proportionate.

### Completion message

Good work. A rights-based next step often begins with better questions: who is affected, who is missing, what barriers exist, what information is needed, and how participation can be safer and more meaningful.
