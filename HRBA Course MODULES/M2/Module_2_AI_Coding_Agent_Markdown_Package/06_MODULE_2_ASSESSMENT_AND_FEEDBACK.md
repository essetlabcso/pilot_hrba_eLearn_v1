# Module 2 Assessment and Feedback Map

## Module 2 assessment rule

All Module 2 checks are **formative only**. They help participants practice recognizing human rights in everyday CSO work, connect rights to practical situations, and prepare for later modules.

Module 2 checks do **not**:

- issue a certificate;
- unlock a certificate;
- replace the final course test;
- rank the participant;
- rank the CSO;
- require sensitive information;
- require document uploads;
- require real cases, complaint records, safeguarding information, or confidential organizational data.

The course certificate remains linked to the **final course test with a score of 80% or above**.

## Feedback rules

Use:

- “That’s right.”
- “Good choice.”
- “Not quite.”

Do not use:

- “Incorrect”
- “Wrong”
- “Failed”
- “Pass/fail”
- “Your CSO is weak”
- “Your organization is non-compliant”
- “This proves your CSO applies HRBA”
- “This affects your certificate”
- “This will be shared with donors”

# M2-A1 — Matching Activity: Everyday Issue to Rights Dimension

## Placement

`M2-S3-03 — Match the Everyday Issue to a Rights Dimension`

## Categories

1. Participation
2. Equality and non-discrimination
3. Access to information
4. Accountability
5. Accessibility of services
6. Safety and dignity

| Item | Example | Correct match | Best-match feedback | Weaker-match feedback |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Community members are invited to a planning meeting only after the project activities have already been decided. | Participation | Good choice. This example is about participation because people affected by a decision should have a meaningful chance to influence that decision early enough. | Not quite. Other rights dimensions may also matter, but the strongest issue here is participation. People are being asked to react after decisions are already made. |
| 2 | A CSO shares project information only in a language that some community members cannot understand. | Access to information | Good choice. This example is about access to information. People need information in a form and language they can understand before they can participate meaningfully. | Not quite. This could also affect participation and inclusion, but the strongest first issue is access to information. |
| 3 | People with disabilities are invited to an event, but the venue is not physically accessible. | Accessibility of services | Good choice. Invitation alone is not enough. Services, meetings, information, and support need to be accessible in practice. | Not quite. This example may also involve equality and participation, but the clearest rights dimension is accessibility. |
| 4 | A project has a feedback box, but people do not know who reads the feedback or whether any action is taken. | Accountability | Good choice. A feedback box is only useful if people know how feedback is handled, receive responses where appropriate, and see that concerns lead to action. | Not quite. Feedback is connected to information and participation, but the strongest issue here is accountability. |
| 5 | A community activity reaches many people, but women, young people, and people from remote areas rarely attend. | Equality and non-discrimination | Good choice. This example asks who may be excluded or benefiting less. HRBA helps CSOs notice and reduce barriers for different groups. | Not quite. The activity may seem successful because many people attend, but the key rights concern is unequal participation or exclusion. |

## Completion message

You have started recognizing rights dimensions in everyday CSO situations. A single issue may involve more than one rights dimension, but identifying the strongest first issue helps CSOs ask better questions.

# M2-A2 — Quick Check: Rights Characteristics

## Placement

`M2-S4-04 — Quick Check: Rights Characteristics`

## Question 1

What does it mean that human rights are universal?

A. Human rights belong only to people who know the law.  
B. Human rights belong to every person because they are human.  
C. Human rights belong only to people selected for a project.  
D. Human rights apply only inside courts.

Best response: B

Feedback for A: Not quite. People do not need legal knowledge to have rights.  
Feedback for B: That’s right. Universal means human rights belong to every person because they are human.  
Feedback for C: Not quite. People do not receive rights because a project selects them. Rights belong to people already.  
Feedback for D: Not quite. Courts can protect rights, but rights also matter in everyday life, services, participation, information, and accountability.

## Question 2

What does it mean that human rights are interdependent?

A. One right can depend on or support the realization of other rights.  
B. Some rights are only for people who already have access to services.  
C. Economic rights are always more important than participation rights.  
D. Human rights apply only when a court case exists.

Best response: A

Feedback for A: That’s right. Rights are connected. For example, meaningful participation may depend on information, safety, language access, mobility, and non-discrimination.  
Feedback for B: Not quite. Rights do not belong only to people who already have access to services.  
Feedback for C: Not quite. HRBA does not rank rights in a simple hierarchy. Different rights can support each other.  
Feedback for D: Not quite. Human rights matter in everyday life, not only when a court case exists.

## Question 3

Which statement best reflects indivisibility?

A. Some rights can be ignored if a project has limited time.  
B. Civil and political rights matter, but social and economic rights do not.  
C. Different rights matter together and should not be treated as completely separate.  
D. CSOs should focus only on the right that is easiest to report.

Best response: C

Feedback for A: Not quite. Limited time may require prioritization, but HRBA should not ignore rights that affect dignity, participation, access, or accountability.  
Feedback for B: Not quite. HRBA recognizes that different types of rights matter and are connected.  
Feedback for C: That’s right. Indivisibility means rights should not be treated as if some categories are irrelevant to people’s real lives.  
Feedback for D: Not quite. Reporting matters, but HRBA starts from people’s rights and barriers, not only what is easiest to report.

# M2-A4 — Quick Check: Using Standards Safely

## Placement

`M2-S6-06 — Quick Check: Using Standards Safely`

## Question

Which is the safest and most practical way for a CSO learner to use human rights standards in this course?

A. Use standards to make unsupported legal claims about a real case.  
B. Use standards to ask better questions about dignity, access, participation, exclusion, responsibility, and accountability.  
C. Use standards to publicly rank other CSOs.  
D. Use standards to replace community participation.

Best response: B

Feedback for A: Not quite. This course supports learning and practical reflection. It does not provide legal advice or ask learners to make claims about real cases.  
Feedback for B: That’s right. Standards can help CSOs ask better practical questions about dignity, access, inclusion, participation, responsibility, and accountability.  
Feedback for C: Not quite. Standards should not be used here to publicly rank or shame CSOs.  
Feedback for D: Not quite. Standards do not replace participation. They should help strengthen participation and accountability.

# M2-A5 — Scenario Decision: The Most Rights-Based Next Step

## Placement

`M2-S7-05 — Scenario Decision: The Most Rights-Based Next Step`

## Scenario

Lomi Community Development Association is planning a consultation on improving access to local services. The team has prepared a meeting agenda and wants to invite community representatives. Ayele notices that some groups are often missing from similar meetings, including people with disabilities, women with care responsibilities, young people, and people from remote areas.

## Question

What is the most rights-based next step?

A. Continue with the existing meeting plan because inviting some representatives is enough.  
B. Pause and ask who may be excluded, what barriers exist, what information people need, and how the meeting can be made more accessible and safe.  
C. Cancel the consultation because inclusion is too difficult.  
D. Collect personal details from every person in the community before making any decision.

Best response: B

Feedback for A: Not quite. Inviting some representatives may be useful, but HRBA asks whether participation is meaningful and who may still be excluded.  
Feedback for B: That’s right. This response applies a rights lens by asking about exclusion, barriers, information, accessibility, safety, and participation before moving ahead.  
Feedback for C: Not quite. Inclusion can be challenging, but HRBA encourages practical adjustments rather than abandoning participation.  
Feedback for D: Not quite. HRBA does not mean collecting as much personal data as possible. Data should be safe, necessary, and proportionate.

# M2-A6 — Final Module 2 Formative Quiz

## Opening text

This short quiz helps you review your learning from Module 2. It is formative and does not affect your certificate.

## Question 1

Which statement best explains human rights in plain language?

A. Human rights are only legal words used by courts and lawyers.  
B. Human rights are basic rights and freedoms connected to dignity, equality, safety, participation, and a decent life.  
C. Human rights are benefits that CSOs give to selected project participants.  
D. Human rights are only relevant to international organizations.

Best response: B

Feedback for A: Not quite. Human rights can be protected through law, but they also matter in everyday life and CSO practice.  
Feedback for B: That’s right. Human rights are basic rights and freedoms connected to dignity, equality, safety, participation, and a decent life.  
Feedback for C: Not quite. Rights are not benefits that CSOs give. People have rights because they are human.  
Feedback for D: Not quite. Human rights are relevant to local CSOs because they affect services, participation, inclusion, accountability, and dignity.

## Question 2

Why should CSOs look for rights dimensions in everyday issues?

A. To make every activity sound legalistic.  
B. To understand dignity, exclusion, access, participation, responsibility, and accountability more clearly.  
C. To avoid speaking with communities.  
D. To replace project planning with legal claims.

Best response: B

Feedback for A: Not quite. HRBA should make CSO work more practical and accountable, not unnecessarily legalistic.  
Feedback for B: That’s right. Looking for rights dimensions helps CSOs ask better questions about dignity, exclusion, access, participation, responsibility, and accountability.  
Feedback for C: Not quite. HRBA strengthens meaningful participation; it does not avoid community engagement.  
Feedback for D: Not quite. Rights analysis should improve project planning, not replace it with unsupported legal claims.

## Question 3

What does it mean that rights are interdependent?

A. One right can depend on or support another right.  
B. Rights only apply if all services are already available.  
C. CSOs can choose one right and ignore all others.  
D. Participation rights are never linked to access to information.

Best response: A

Feedback for A: That’s right. Rights are connected. For example, participation may depend on information, safety, accessibility, and non-discrimination.  
Feedback for B: Not quite. Rights matter even when services are not fully available.  
Feedback for C: Not quite. HRBA encourages CSOs to notice how different rights and barriers connect.  
Feedback for D: Not quite. Participation is often closely linked to access to information.

## Question 4

What is the best way to understand types of rights in CSO work?

A. Rights types are useful learning categories, but real issues often involve several rights at the same time.  
B. Each issue can involve only one right.  
C. Social rights matter, but participation and accountability do not.  
D. Rights categories should be used to avoid deeper analysis.

Best response: A

Feedback for A: That’s right. Rights types help organize thinking, but everyday issues often involve several rights together.  
Feedback for B: Not quite. One issue can involve multiple rights and barriers.  
Feedback for C: Not quite. HRBA recognizes the importance of participation, accountability, equality, and many types of rights.  
Feedback for D: Not quite. Rights categories should support deeper analysis, not replace it.

## Question 5

How should a learner use human rights standards in Module 2?

A. As legal advice for real disputes.  
B. As practical guidance for asking better questions about rights, barriers, responsibilities, and accountability.  
C. As a way to expose confidential organizational information.  
D. As a reason to skip community participation.

Best response: B

Feedback for A: Not quite. This course supports learning and practical reflection. It does not provide legal advice.  
Feedback for B: That’s right. Standards can guide practical questions about rights, barriers, responsibilities, participation, and accountability.  
Feedback for C: Not quite. Learners should not expose confidential information, real case details, complaints, or sensitive data.  
Feedback for D: Not quite. Standards should strengthen participation, not replace it.
