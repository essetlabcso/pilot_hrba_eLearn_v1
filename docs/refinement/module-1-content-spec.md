## Module purpose

Module 1 introduces learners to the HRBA learning journey. It should not teach the full foundations of HRBA in detail. Its purpose is to help local CSO learners understand why HRBA matters, what they will practice, how the course will work, and how to begin noticing rights, exclusion, responsibility, participation, and accountability in ordinary CSO work.

## Module-level learning objectives

By the end of this module, learners will be able to:

1. Explain HRBA in simple, practical language.
2. Recognize that ordinary CSO work can involve rights, dignity, inclusion, participation, and accountability.
3. Notice who may be less visible or left at the edge of a project activity.
4. Distinguish between people as beneficiaries and people as rights-holders.
5. Identify basic responsibility questions for CSO practice.
6. Make one safe personal learning commitment for applying an HRBA lens.

## Safe-learning rule for all reflection fields

All learner reflection prompts must include or imply this rule:

Keep your examples broad and safe. Do not include real names, active complaints, safeguarding details, legal disputes, confidential organizational records, beneficiary lists, or politically sensitive information.

## M1-S03 — Why This Module Matters

### Main title
Why This Module Matters

### Intro
Many CSO activities look successful on paper. A meeting is held. A water point is built. A training is completed. A report is submitted.

But HRBA asks a deeper question:

### Core question
Did the work respect people’s dignity, voice, inclusion, participation, and accountability?

### Four value cards
1. **Exclusion can hide inside success.**  
   A project may reach its target and still miss people who face barriers.

2. **Responsibility matters.**  
   HRBA helps us ask who has duties, who has influence, and what role a CSO can safely play.

3. **Participation is more than attendance.**  
   People should have meaningful ways to understand, speak, influence, and receive a response.

4. **Feedback should lead to action.**  
   A complaint box or meeting is not enough if people never hear what changed.

### Closing line
This module helps you begin seeing those issues earlier.

## M1-S05 — Water Project Story

### Main title
A Water Project Story

### Scenario
A local CSO supports a community water point. The project report says the construction was completed and the water point is now available.

At first, this sounds like a successful activity.

Later, the team hears different experiences:

- Some households say they did not know how the water point location was selected.
- Some women say the queue is difficult because they also carry care responsibilities.
- An older person says the path is hard to use.
- A youth group says they were never asked about safety around the site.
- A community member says complaints were raised but no one explained what happened next.

### Prompt
What do you notice?

### Learner instruction
Select the points in the scene to hear different perspectives.

## M1-S06 — Investigate the HRBA Lens

### Main title
Investigate the HRBA Lens

### Intro
The water point story is not only about infrastructure. It is also about people’s dignity, access, information, participation, safety, and accountability.

### Lens cards
1. **Information**  
   Who understood the decision, criteria, and process?

2. **Access**  
   Who can actually use the service safely and regularly?

3. **Voice**  
   Who was asked, who spoke, and who influenced the decision?

4. **Inclusion**  
   Who may face extra barriers or remain less visible?

5. **Responsibility**  
   Who has a role in addressing the issue?

6. **Response**  
   What happens after people raise concerns?

### Closing line
An HRBA lens helps a CSO look beyond delivery and ask how people experience the work.

## M1-S07 — So, What Is HRBA?

### Main title
So, What Is HRBA?

### Simple explanation
HRBA means using human rights standards and principles to guide how development work is planned, implemented, monitored, and improved.

In CSO practice, HRBA helps us ask:

- Who has rights in this situation?
- Who has duties or responsibilities?
- Who may be excluded?
- How can people participate meaningfully?
- How can decisions be more transparent and accountable?
- How can action be safe, practical, and respectful?

### Practical wording
HRBA is not only legal language. It is a way of working that places people’s dignity, equality, participation, and accountability at the center.

### Bridge note
You will explore the full foundation of HRBA in Module 2. For now, keep this simple idea: HRBA helps a CSO look beyond activities and see people, power, responsibility, and rights.

## M1-S08 — Everyday CSO Work

### Main title
Everyday CSO Work

### Intro
Rights issues can appear in many areas of local CSO work. They are not limited to courts, legal cases, or advocacy campaigns.

2. **Health**

3. **Water**

4. **Protection and safety**  
   Who faces risk, fear, stigma, or unsafe treatment?

5. **Livelihoods**  
   Who controls opportunities, income, decisions, and benefits?

6. **Voice and participation**  
   Who can speak, influence, question, and receive a response?

### Closing line

## M1-S09 — Who Might Be Invisible?

### Main title
Who Might Be Invisible?

### Intro
A CSO may say “the community participated,” but a community is not one single group. Different people may face different barriers.

### Perspective cards
1. **Women and girls**  
   Are their time, safety, care responsibilities, and voice considered?

2. **Men and boys**  
   Are their roles, risks, expectations, and participation understood without assumptions?

3. **Older people**  
   Can they access the activity, information, and support?

4. **People with disabilities**  
   Are physical, communication, attitude, and participation barriers addressed?

5. **Youth and children**  
   Are their views considered safely and appropriately?

6. **Less visible groups**  
   Who may be missed because they are not connected to leaders, committees, or usual meeting spaces?

### Closing line
HRBA helps us ask who is present, who is missing, and who is present but not heard.

## M1-S10 — Rights Are Connected

### Main title
Rights Are Connected

### Intro
One issue can connect to several rights at the same time.

### Example
A water point may connect to:

- health, because unsafe water affects wellbeing;
- education, because children may miss school when water collection takes time;
- dignity, because people should access services respectfully;
- participation, because people should influence decisions that affect them;
- accountability, because concerns should receive a response.

### Core message
Rights are connected. A practical CSO response should look beyond the first visible need and ask what barriers affect people’s lives.

## M1-S11 — From Beneficiaries to Rights-Holders

### Main title
From Beneficiaries to Rights-Holders

### Intro
In CSO work, we often use words like beneficiaries, target groups, service users, or community members. These words can be useful for planning, but they can also make people sound passive.

### Shift
HRBA asks us to also see people as rights-holders.

### Comparison cards
**Beneficiary lens**  
People receive support from a project.

**Rights-holder lens**  
People have dignity, claims, voice, agency, and a right to be treated fairly.

### Practical meaning
This does not mean a CSO promises to solve every problem. It means the CSO works in a way that respects people’s dignity and strengthens their ability to participate, ask questions, and seek response.

## M1-S12 — Who Has Responsibility?

### Main title
Who Has Responsibility?

### Intro
HRBA does not mean the CSO must solve everything alone. It helps clarify who has responsibility, who has influence, and what role the CSO can safely play.

### Actor cards
1. **Rights-holders**  
   People and groups whose rights, dignity, access, participation, or accountability are affected.

2. **Duty-bearers**  
   Actors with formal obligations, often public authorities or institutions.

3. **Supporting actors**  
   CSOs, community structures, service providers, donors, networks, and others who can support change.

4. **The CSO role**  
   A CSO may inform, facilitate, connect, support participation, document patterns safely, advocate, or strengthen accountability.

### Closing line
A rights-based CSO does not take over everyone’s role. It helps the right actors see and act on the right responsibilities.

## M1-S13 — Participation Is More Than Attendance

### Main title
Participation Is More Than Attendance

### Intro
Attendance can be counted. Participation must be understood.

### Participation ladder
1. **Present**  
   People are in the room or listed on the attendance sheet.

2. **Informed**  
   People understand the purpose, options, criteria, and process.

3. **Heard**  
   People can speak, ask questions, and raise concerns safely.

4. **Considered**  
   The team reviews what people said and explains how it shaped decisions.

5. **Influential**  
   People’s views can affect priorities, design, follow-up, or accountability.

### Core message
Meaningful participation is not proven by attendance alone.

## M1-S14 — From Services to Rights, Power, and Accountability

### Main title
From Services to Rights, Power, and Accountability

### Intro
The same project can be seen in two different ways.

### Service-only question
Did we deliver the activity?

### HRBA questions
- Who was affected differently?
- Who may still be excluded?
- Who had information?
- Who could participate safely?
- Who had influence?
- Who has responsibility?
- What response did people receive?
- What should change next time?

### Core message
This shift in analysis is what makes HRBA practical.

## M1-S15 — Module 1 Knowledge Check

### Main title
Module 1 Knowledge Check

### Intro
Use what you have practiced. Choose the strongest answer, not the easiest answer.

### Question 1
A CSO reports: “The community attended the consultation.” What is the strongest HRBA follow-up question?

A. Was the attendance sheet signed?  
B. Who attended, who was missing, and who could influence the discussion?  
C. Was the meeting held on time?  
D. Did the facilitator speak clearly?

Correct answer: B  
Feedback: HRBA looks beyond attendance and asks who was included, who was heard, and who could influence decisions.

### Question 2
Which statement best describes a rights-holder?

A. A person who receives project support and should be grateful.  
B. A person or group with dignity, rights, voice, and legitimate claims.  
C. A person selected by the project committee.  
D. A person who has legal training.

Correct answer: B  
Feedback: A rights-holder is not passive. HRBA recognizes people as having dignity, agency, rights, and claims.

### Question 3
A project delivers a water point, but some people cannot use it safely. What does an HRBA lens help the CSO ask?

A. Whether the activity should be removed from the report.  
B. Whether access, safety, participation, and accountability barriers remain.  
C. Whether the construction team worked quickly.  
D. Whether the project should avoid community feedback.

Correct answer: B  
Feedback: HRBA helps teams look beyond delivery and examine how people experience the service.
