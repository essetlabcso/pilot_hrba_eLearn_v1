# Worked Example Library Draft

All examples below are fictional, generalized, and non-sensitive. They are draft review copy, not implementation-ready source text until HRBA, safeguarding, and instructional design reviewers approve them.

## Example 1: Module 2 Matching Rights Characteristics

- Screen/cluster: M2-S06 / Match Rights Characteristics
- Learning purpose: Help learners see why a practical situation connects to a rights characteristic.
- Worked example scenario: A project meeting notice is shared one day before the meeting, and some members hear about it only after decisions are made.
- Strong choice or repaired version: Match this to participation and information access, not only attendance.
- Why this is stronger: The issue is not just whether a meeting happened. People need timely information and a real chance to influence decisions.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Use a generalized meeting example. Do not describe a real dispute, official, or identifiable group.
- Transition to learner task: Now match the next examples by asking, "What did people need in order to participate fairly and safely?"

## Example 2: Module 2 Meaningful Participation Rating

- Screen/cluster: M2-S15 / Participation Rating
- Learning purpose: Model how to rate participation beyond presence.
- Worked example scenario: A committee invites community members to attend, but the agenda is already decided and questions are only taken at the end.
- Strong choice or repaired version: Rate this as limited participation. A stronger version would share the agenda early, invite input before the decision, provide accessible ways to comment, and explain what changed because of feedback.
- Why this is stronger: Meaningful participation includes information, influence, safe voice, and response.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Do not name a real committee, village, or decision. Keep the example broad.
- Transition to learner task: Now rate each scenario by looking for information, access, voice, influence, and feedback.

## Example 3: Module 2 Accountability And Feedback Loop

- Screen/cluster: M2-S16 and M2-S17 / Accountability Loop and Feedback Repair
- Learning purpose: Show that a feedback channel is not enough without response and follow-up.
- Worked example scenario: A suggestion box exists, but people do not know who reads it or whether comments stay private.
- Strong choice or repaired version: Explain what can be raised, who reviews feedback, how privacy is protected, when a general response will be shared, and how repeated issues will lead to process changes.
- Why this is stronger: It turns a passive channel into a loop: receive, review, respond, adapt, and document follow-up.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Do not quote real complaints or identify anyone who raised a concern.
- Transition to learner task: Now repair each broken loop by asking what is missing: clarity, safe use, review, response, or adaptation.

## Example 4: Module 2 Power And Exclusion Pathway

- Screen/cluster: M2-S18 and M2-S19 / Power, Exclusion, and Pathway Thinking
- Learning purpose: Help learners connect a barrier to a participation effect and a decision effect.
- Worked example scenario: Information is shared through one informal network, so people outside that network hear late and are less prepared to speak.
- Strong choice or repaired version: Barrier: late information. Participation effect: some people cannot prepare questions. Decision effect: the final choice reflects the views of people who heard early. Safer action: use more than one information channel and check who may still be missed.
- Why this is stronger: It shows how power can shape decisions without blaming individuals.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Avoid naming real networks, leaders, or excluded groups.
- Transition to learner task: Now trace one pathway from barrier to decision effect, then choose a safe action.

## Example 5: Module 2 Portfolio And Knowledge-Check Preparation

- Screen/cluster: M2-S21 and M2-S22 / Everyday Rights Lens Portfolio and Knowledge Check
- Learning purpose: Model a safe, specific portfolio output before assessment-like reflection.
- Worked example scenario: "In a fictional water activity, some members may not hear meeting information early enough to participate."
- Strong choice or repaired version: "One safe improvement I could test is to share meeting information through two channels and ask whether people understood the decision point before the meeting."
- Why this is stronger: It names one barrier, one practical improvement, and one follow-up check.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Do not include names, exact locations, real complaints, or confidential project details.
- Transition to learner task: Now choose one generalized participation or accountability improvement you could safely test.

## Example 6: Module 3 Actor Mapping

- Screen/cluster: M3-S1-08 / Design Actor Map
- Learning purpose: Model role clarity among rights-holders, duty-bearers, influence actors, and CSO support.
- Worked example scenario: A fictional service activity has low participation from people who live farther from the meeting site.
- Strong choice or repaired version: Rights-holders: people affected by the service decision. Duty-bearer: the responsible public service or sector office. Influence actors: local committee members and service providers. CSO role: support safe participation, gather non-sensitive evidence, and facilitate dialogue.
- Why this is stronger: It keeps responsibility clear and avoids making the CSO responsible for everything.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Do not name real offices, officials, or committees.
- Transition to learner task: Now map actors by asking who has rights, who has formal responsibility, who influences access, and what the CSO can safely support.

## Example 7: Module 3 Root-Cause And Capacity-Gap Tree

- Screen/cluster: M3-S1-10 / Root-Cause and Capacity-Gap Tree
- Learning purpose: Distinguish symptoms, root causes, capacity gaps, and participation risks.
- Worked example scenario: Symptom: attendance is low. Possible root cause: meeting times conflict with care work and travel. Capacity gap: the team has not checked timing or access barriers. Participation risk: decisions reflect only those who can attend.
- Strong choice or repaired version: Design implication: test accessible timing, use more than one information channel, and ask for feedback on whether participation felt possible.
- Why this is stronger: It shifts from blaming people for not attending to examining barriers and responsibilities.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Avoid suggesting that any group is uninterested or unwilling.
- Transition to learner task: Now build one branch from symptom to root cause, capacity gap, and design implication.

## Example 8: Module 3 Objective Repair

- Screen/cluster: M3-S1-13 and M3-S1-14 / Objective Repair
- Learning purpose: Show that HRBA objectives include barriers, participation, responsibility, and accountability.
- Worked example scenario: Weak objective: "Train 100 participants on service use."
- Strong choice or repaired version: Stronger objective: "Improve fair access to service information and feedback by reducing timing and communication barriers and supporting responsible actors to respond to concerns."
- Why this is stronger: It looks beyond activity delivery and names access, feedback, and responsible response.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Do not promise that the CSO alone will fulfil rights or fix all service barriers.
- Transition to learner task: Now repair the objective by adding the barrier, rights-holder perspective, responsible actor, and accountability check.

## Example 9: Module 3 Activity Package And Intervention Logic

- Screen/cluster: M3-S1-16 and M3-S1-16A / Activity Package and Intervention Logic
- Learning purpose: Move from isolated activities to coherent HRBA action.
- Worked example scenario: If people miss information because notices are shared too late, a training alone will not solve the barrier.
- Strong choice or repaired version: Activity package: adjust information channels, hold accessible consultation, agree on response roles with the responsible actor, and document follow-up. Logic: better information and safer voice should improve participation and accountability.
- Why this is stronger: Activities link to the barrier and include feedback and responsibility.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Check confidentiality, backlash, and access risks before engagement.
- Transition to learner task: Now test whether each activity responds to a barrier and has a safe follow-up route.

## Example 10: Module 3 Risk And Evidence Choices

- Screen/cluster: M3-S1-17 and M3-S1-18 / Risk Plan and Evidence Choices
- Learning purpose: Model proportionate evidence and do-no-harm thinking.
- Worked example scenario: A team hears that some people avoid a feedback channel because they are worried about being identified.
- Strong choice or repaired version: Safer evidence: ask for generalized patterns through a confidential channel and review whether people understand privacy. Risk action: do not share raw comments or names; report only broad themes.
- Why this is stronger: It protects rights-holders while still using evidence to improve the project.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Do not collect identifying details unless there is a clear, safe, approved reason.
- Transition to learner task: Now choose evidence that is useful, proportionate, and safe.

## Example 11: Module 3 Challenge And Portfolio Snapshot

- Screen/cluster: M3-S1-20 and M3-S1-22 / Module Challenge and Portfolio Snapshot
- Learning purpose: Show what a compact project design snapshot can look like.
- Worked example scenario: Fictional issue: service information reaches some people late.
- Strong choice or repaired version: Snapshot: barrier is late information; rights-holder concern is uneven participation; duty-bearer link is responsible service communication; CSO support is safe evidence and dialogue; follow-up is a general response on what changed.
- Why this is stronger: It is specific enough to guide action but avoids sensitive details.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Use fictional or generalized examples only.
- Transition to learner task: Now build your own safe snapshot using one barrier, one actor link, one safe action, and one follow-up.

## Example 12: Module 4 Implementation Warning Signs And Access Repair

- Screen/cluster: M4-S1-04 and M4-S1-06 / Implementation Lens and Access Repair
- Learning purpose: Help learners notice warning signs during delivery.
- Worked example scenario: Participation appears high, but the same people speak each time and others receive information late.
- Strong choice or repaired version: Warning sign: participation is visible but influence may be narrow. Repair: vary information channels, check timing and access barriers, and ask whether people understand what decision is being made.
- Why this is stronger: It keeps implementation responsive while the project is running.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Do not single out individuals or publicly blame participants.
- Transition to learner task: Now identify the warning sign and choose a safe repair.

## Example 13: Module 4 Feedback Response

- Screen/cluster: M4-S1-07 / Feedback Response
- Learning purpose: Model safe loop closure during implementation.
- Worked example scenario: Several general comments suggest that meeting times are difficult for some participants.
- Strong choice or repaired version: Verify the pattern, assess whether changing timing creates risks for others, test an alternative time, and share back in general terms what changed and why.
- Why this is stronger: It treats feedback as evidence and closes the loop without exposing anyone.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Do not quote or identify feedback providers.
- Transition to learner task: Now choose the response that verifies, protects, engages, and follows up.

## Example 14: Module 4 Actor Engagement And Adaptation Without Harm

- Screen/cluster: M4-S1-08 and M4-S1-09 / Actor Engagement and Adaptation
- Learning purpose: Keep CSO role bounded while supporting safe adaptation.
- Worked example scenario: A project team notices that a service barrier requires action by a responsible public actor.
- Strong choice or repaired version: The CSO prepares non-sensitive pattern evidence, discusses the issue constructively with the responsible actor, supports rights-holder participation where safe, and documents agreed follow-up.
- Why this is stronger: It supports accountability without replacing the duty-bearer or making unsafe public accusations.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Assess risk before engagement and avoid naming people in shared evidence.
- Transition to learner task: Now choose an adaptation that is practical, role-clear, and safe.

## Example 15: Module 4 Portfolio Adjustment Note

- Screen/cluster: M4-S1-10 / Portfolio Adjustment Note
- Learning purpose: Model a usable, safe implementation note.
- Worked example scenario: "In a fictional delivery activity, information may not reach people who cannot attend the usual meeting time."
- Strong choice or repaired version: "Signal: late or missed information. Safe evidence: general attendance and feedback pattern. Adjustment: test another information channel. Follow-up: share a general update on what changed."
- Why this is stronger: It is practical, specific, and non-identifying.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Do not include names, complaints, exact locations, or internal disputes.
- Transition to learner task: Now write one safe adjustment note using a generalized example.

## Example 16: Module 5 Evidence Quality

- Screen/cluster: M5-S1-05 / Evidence Classification
- Learning purpose: Help learners classify evidence by what it can and cannot show.
- Worked example scenario: Attendance records show how many people came, but not whether different people could influence decisions.
- Strong choice or repaired version: Classify attendance records as useful activity/access evidence, then pair them with safe feedback or observation patterns to understand participation quality.
- Why this is stronger: It avoids treating numbers alone as proof of rights-based change.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Use aggregated records and avoid identifying who attended or did not attend.
- Transition to learner task: Now classify each evidence item by what it can show and what it cannot show.

## Example 17: Module 5 Indicator Repair

- Screen/cluster: M5-S1-06 and M5-S1-07 / Indicator Repair
- Learning purpose: Show how to move from output indicators to HRBA MEAL indicators.
- Worked example scenario: Weak indicator: "Number of awareness sessions held."
- Strong choice or repaired version: Stronger set: number of sessions held; percentage of participants receiving information in accessible formats; number of feedback themes reviewed; documented adjustments made after feedback.
- Why this is stronger: It balances output, access, participation, accountability, and adaptation.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Keep indicators at aggregated level and collect only the detail needed.
- Transition to learner task: Now repair the indicator set by adding one access, one participation/accountability, and one change signal.

## Example 18: Module 5 Safer Disaggregation

- Screen/cluster: M5-S1-09 / Safer Disaggregation
- Learning purpose: Show that more detail is not always safer or better.
- Worked example scenario: A very small subgroup appears in a report table, and the combination of age, disability, and location could identify one household.
- Strong choice or repaired version: Aggregate the data, remove the risky combination, or describe the barrier pattern without publishing identifying detail.
- Why this is stronger: It protects people while still allowing the project to learn from patterns.
- Safety / safeguarding note: If a category could point to one person or household, aggregate or avoid reporting it.
- Transition to learner task: Now choose the least detailed option that still supports safe learning.

## Example 19: Module 5 Ethical Storytelling And Donor Story Request

- Screen/cluster: M5-S1-12 and M5-S1-13 / Ethical Storytelling and Risky Story Request
- Learning purpose: Model a safe alternative to an individual success story.
- Worked example scenario: A donor asks for a named story from a person affected by a sensitive service barrier.
- Strong choice or repaired version: "We cannot share an identifiable story. We can provide an anonymized pattern, aggregated feedback, and a short composite example reviewed for safety."
- Why this is stronger: It respects dignity, consent, privacy, and power differences while still giving useful evidence.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Consent is not enough if there is pressure, dependency, or risk of identification.
- Transition to learner task: Now choose the response that protects people and offers safe evidence.

## Example 20: Module 5 Responsible Reporting

- Screen/cluster: M5-S1-16 and M5-S1-17 / Responsible Reporting and Risky Claims
- Learning purpose: Model safe evidence-based reporting language.
- Worked example scenario: Risky claim: "Local leaders ignored the needs of excluded people."
- Strong choice or repaired version: Safer claim: "Feedback and participation records suggest some people had less access to information and fewer chances to influence decisions. The team will verify the pattern and discuss safe adjustments with responsible actors."
- Why this is stronger: It avoids unsupported blame and points to evidence, risk review, engagement, and follow-up.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Do not name officials, communities, or complainants in learning examples.
- Transition to learner task: Now spot the risky claim and choose a safer evidence-based version.

## Example 21: Module 5 Portfolio Improvement Plan And 90-Day Action Plan

- Screen/cluster: M5-S1-20 and M5-S1-22 / Portfolio Improvement Plan and 90-Day Action Plan
- Learning purpose: Show a practical transfer plan that is specific but safe.
- Worked example scenario: Fictional MEAL issue: feedback is collected but not reviewed for access barriers.
- Strong choice or repaired version: 30 days: review general feedback themes for access barriers. 60 days: test one safer feedback channel. 90 days: document what changed and share a general response with participants.
- Why this is stronger: It links evidence, action, accountability, and follow-up without exposing anyone.
- Safety / safeguarding note: Do not include sensitive complaints, names, or internal disputes.
- Transition to learner task: Now write one feasible 90-day action using one safe evidence source and one follow-up moment.
